3.1 Comparison-Oriented Representation Framework
Prior work has shown that recipes can be represented as structured pro￾cedural knowledge rather than as flat linear text. Existing state-based,
graph-based, and program-like models capture ingredients, actions,
intermediate products, dependencies, and process ordering in recipe
procedures [28, 30, 36]. Building on these foundations, we represent
each recipe as a comparison-oriented state–action process graph, in
which states denote ingredient, intermediate, or cooking-environment
conditions, and actions denote operations that transform, combine, or
relocate them. This representation provides the foundation for our
comparison framework and visualization.
3.1.1 State-Action Modeling
Based on prior work, we model each recipe as a state–action process
graph. This representation externalizes causal structure, intermediate
results, and state transitions that are only implicit in recipe text, sup￾porting both recipe understanding and cross-recipe comparison. As
shown in Fig. 2, nodes represent states and links represent actions. A
state denotes the condition of an ingredient, intermediate food entity,
or cooking environment at a particular stage of the recipe. An action
denotes an operation that transforms a state, combines multiple states,
or moves a state into a new cooking context. We instantiate a new state
whenever an action introduces a change that is semantically relevant to
subsequent steps.
State attributes. Each state is described by the following at￾tributes:
1. container, the associated cooking environment, such as none,
wok, pan, or pot;
2. name, the ingredient or intermediate identity;
3. type, the component role, defined as main, auxiliary, or con￾tainer;
4. physical_state, the physical form of the entity, categorized as
liquid, paste, solid, powder, or small pieces;
5. chemical_state, a coarse-grained transformation status, encoded
as raw or cooked to support stable comparison across recipe
variants;
6. flavor, the accumulated flavor characteristics inherited from added
ingredients, seasonings, and prior transformations; and
7. provenance, the set of incoming source-state/action pairs that
structurally derive the current state.
Transition attributes. Each transition represents an action that
maps one or more input states to an output state in the recipe graph. A
transition contains:
1. action, the operation applied in the step;
2. type, the broader process category, defined as prep, hot process,
or cold process;
3. inputs, the input state or set of input states;
4. outputs, the resulting state or set of output states.
To illustrate the representation, consider the red-braised pork belly
recipe in Fig. 2. The instruction “cut the pork belly into evenly sized
cubes” is encoded as a transition from MEAT (raw, whole) to MEAT
(raw, cube) via cut, changing physical_state while preserving chemi￾cal_state. The instruction “put the pork into a pot of cold water and
blanch” moves the meat into a pot context and produces a new blanched
meat state, updating both container and chemical_state. In a parallel
branch, “heat oil in a wok and add rock sugar” establishes a wok￾centered cooking context. When the blanched meat later enters this
branch and is fried, the result is represented as a new intermediate state
whose provenance records the convergence of multiple prior states and
actions.
3.1.2 State Transition Mechanisms
More specifically, we group action-induced state transitions into two
structural types: (1) single-ingredient processing and (2) multi￾ingredient combination. In single-ingredient processing, a state
changes while remaining tied to one ingredient throughout the pro￾cess (Fig. 2a). In multi-ingredient combination, separately processed
ingredient states are brought together to form a new intermediate state
that continues as a subsequent process unit (Fig. 2b). Together, these
two types produce a multi-branch process structure in which ingredient
states may evolve independently before later converging.
Across both types, transitions may occur through either physical
transitions or heat-mediated transitions. Physical transitions take
place without a heated cooking environment, whereas heat-mediated
transitions occur within a cooking environment such as a wok, pan, or
pot (Fig. 2c).
We distinguish physical combination from heat-mediated cooking
because their semantics differ. In physical combination, the mixing
order is often less important, and the container is usually not central
to the resulting state. For example, when flour and water are mixed
into dough, the key result is the combined state rather than the vessel
used. In heat-mediated cooking, however, both the order of ingredient
introduction and the site of interaction can substantially affect the
intermediate and final outcomes. This distinction motivates our explicit
representation of the cooking environment as a separate node, which
captures both the container and the sequence in which ingredients enter
it.
Finally, we distinguish ingredient states by functional role: main
ingredient states and auxiliary ingredient states (Fig. 2d). Main
ingredient states define the dish’s primary identity and transformation
path, whereas auxiliary ingredient states contribute aroma, seasoning,
flavor balance, or other supporting functions. This distinction helps
clarify the hierarchical organization of recipe components and the roles
different branches play in the overall process.
3.1.3 Comparison-Oriented Structural
Building on the state and transition attributes defined above, recipe
comparison is formulated as the alignment of similar process elements
across two recipe graphs. At a high level, this involves identifying corre￾spondences between states, actions, and their local transition structures.
Since states summarize ingredient identity, intermediate condition, and
process stage, they serve as the primary anchors for alignment; actions
are then evaluated based on whether they connect matched or compat￾ible states in comparable ways. The comparison procedure proceeds
in two stages. We first identify candidate state correspondences across
the two graphs by comparing state attributes; we then examine the
transitions connecting matched states to determine whether the linked
operations express comparable process semantics. The effective seman￾tic unit of comparison is thus the local state–action transition pattern
rather than isolated nodes or edges.
Matching rules. We define state matching as a semantic corre￾spondence jointly determined by five attributes: provenance, name,
type, chemical_state, and physical_state. Among them, name and
type provide the essential identity of a state, while chemical_state and
physical_state describe its current condition, and provenance captures
its incoming structural provenance.
Based on these attributes, we distinguish three forms of state corre￾spondence:
(1) Exact. Two states are considered exact when all five attributes
are identical. This indicates that they represent the same ingredient role
and condition under the same incoming structural history.

(2) Similar. Two states are considered similar when they share the
same name and type, but do not satisfy exact correspondence. This
indicates that they remain semantically aligned in ingredient identity
and role, while differing in condition or provenance.
Because multiple candidate pairs may satisfy the similar condition
for the same state, similarity alone is not sufficient to determine which
pair should be aligned first. To ensure that each state is matched
with the most semantically appropriate counterpart, we introduce a
priority-based matching strategy. The priority is determined by the
relative importance of the remaining matched attributes, ordered as
chemical_state = physical_state > provenance. Thus, among state
pairs that already agree in name and type, those that preserve more
condition and provenance information are preferred. In particular, a
pair receives higher priority when its provenance attributes, although
not globally identical, still preserve the same most recent incoming
source–action relation. During alignment, higher-priority similar pairs
are assigned first, and lower-priority pairs are considered only after
stronger correspondences have been exhausted.
(3) Different. If either name or type does not match, the two states
are treated as different.
